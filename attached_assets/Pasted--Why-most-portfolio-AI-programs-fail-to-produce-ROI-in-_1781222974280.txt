# Why most portfolio AI programs fail to produce ROI in 12 months



Most private equity firms are not short on AI ambition.



They are short on a repeatable way to turn that ambition into portfolio-level value inside a hold period.



That gap shows up fast. One portfolio company buys copilots. Another launches a forecasting pilot. A third hires a consultant to write an AI roadmap. Six months later, the firm has more vendors, more experiments, and more board slides, but not much it can tie to EBITDA, productivity, or time-to-decision.



This is where many portfolio AI programs stall. Not because AI lacks potential, but because the operating model around it is weak.



If you are the operating partner or portfolio value creation leader carrying the mandate, the goal is not to prove that AI matters. The market already settled that. The goal is to build an AI program that produces measurable outcomes, with governance and adoption strong enough to survive contact with real operators.



Here are four failure modes we see most often.



## 1. Fragmented pilots create activity, not portfolio value



At the portfolio level, AI often starts as a series of local experiments.



That sounds reasonable. Let management teams test use cases close to the business. Keep the barrier to entry low. Move quickly.



The problem is that fragmented pilots rarely compound.



One company pilots customer support automation. Another tests demand forecasting. Another experiments with internal productivity tools. Each initiative may be valid on its own, but without a common value-creation lens, the firm ends up funding disconnected work that cannot be compared, prioritized, or scaled.



This creates three practical problems:



- no shared definition of ROI

- no cross-portfolio learning loop

- no clear basis for deciding which pilots deserve more capital



The result is motion without a portfolio thesis.



A stronger approach is to define a small set of AI value categories across the portfolio first, revenue acceleration, margin improvement, working capital efficiency, decision speed, and labor productivity. Then each pilot is evaluated against the same business outcomes, not just technical feasibility.



AI should not be managed as a collection of interesting tests. It should be managed like a value-creation portfolio.



## 2. Duplicated vendor spend quietly erodes returns



This is one of the least discussed problems in portfolio AI.



When each company explores AI independently, vendor sprawl follows. Multiple companies buy overlapping tools. Teams pay for licenses they barely use. Advisors propose similar assessments in different language. Procurement happens locally, but the cost of duplication accumulates at the fund level.



At the same time, the AI market is moving quickly. Model pricing is shifting, product bundles are changing, and major platforms are still redefining what is included versus what requires separate spend. That makes ad hoc buying even riskier.



Without a portfolio-wide view, firms end up paying for experimentation twice, once in software, and again in management distraction.



This is not an argument for forcing every company onto one stack. Portfolio companies have different realities, data environments, and risk profiles.



It is an argument for tighter investment discipline:



- standardize how AI tools are evaluated

- identify where shared vendors or partners make sense

- separate enterprise-grade needs from curiosity-driven purchases

- review utilization before expanding licenses or pilots



Good AI strategy is not vendor-first. It is operating-model first.



## 3. Weak governance slows adoption and raises risk



Many firms think governance becomes important later, after the first wins.



In practice, weak governance is one reason the first wins never scale.



When no one has defined approved use cases, review standards, data boundaries, or role ownership, teams hesitate. Legal gets nervous. IT pushes back. Operators create workarounds. Leaders hear mixed signals and lose confidence in the program.



The issue is not that companies need heavy bureaucracy. They need publishable guardrails.



That means clear standards for where AI can be used, how outputs are reviewed, what data can be exposed, and who is accountable for final decisions. In our own work, the principle is simple: AI accelerates, experts decide.



That is why every meaningful AI program needs human review built in. Nothing important should move without it. Not because teams are behind, and not because AI is useless, but because sustainable adoption depends on trust.



A practical governance model should answer four questions:



### What is allowed

Define approved use cases by function and risk level.



### Who owns it

Assign business owners, not just technical owners.



### How it gets reviewed

Use simple workflows that make human review visible and consistent.



### How learning gets captured

Document what worked, what failed, and what should be repeated across the portfolio.



A lot of firms treat governance as a brake. The better view is that governance is what lets you move faster without creating unforced errors.



## 4. No operator-owned roadmap means no durable ROI



This is usually the biggest issue.



Too many AI programs are owned by innovation teams, IT leaders, or outside advisors, while the operators who own the P&L stay on the sidelines.



That creates a familiar pattern. The roadmap sounds promising in the boardroom, but it never becomes part of how the company actually runs. Use cases are interesting, yet they are not tied to line ownership, operating cadence, or measurable milestones.



In PE, that is fatal.



If an AI initiative is supposed to improve gross margin, reduce service cost, speed quoting, or improve forecasting accuracy, the roadmap needs to be owned by the leader responsible for that outcome. Not adjacent to them. Not loosely sponsored by them. Owned by them.



This is where many firms need a more disciplined translation layer between AI strategy and execution. Not a vague transformation agenda, and not a giant systems program. A practical roadmap that connects:



- the business outcome

- the use case

- the data and workflow requirements

- the accountable operator

- the 30, 60, and 90-day milestones

- the measure of success



That is how AI becomes part of value creation, instead of a side project.



## What a better portfolio AI program looks like



The firms getting traction tend to do four things differently.



### They start with the value thesis

They define where AI can move EBITDA, speed, or growth across the portfolio before approving scattered experiments.



### They assess readiness honestly

They look at data maturity, workflow readiness, leadership alignment, and change capacity, not just tooling.



### They build governance early

They create clear guardrails that make adoption safer and faster.



### They assign operator ownership

They tie each initiative to a business leader, a measurable outcome, and a short value-realization timeline.



This is also where boutique, senior-led advisory has an advantage over large, technology-first programs. The issue is rarely access to more tools. It is aligning leadership, operating rhythm, and accountability so the right tools produce real outcomes.



A portfolio company does not become AI-first because it bought software. It becomes AI-first when leadership can decide where AI matters, govern it responsibly, and build it into how the business operates.



## The next 12 months will reward discipline, not enthusiasm



AI is getting cheaper, faster, and easier to test. That is good news for operators. It also means the penalty for weak discipline will rise.



More pilots will get launched. More tools will get bought. More management teams will say they are doing AI.



Very few will be able to show what changed in the P&L.



For private equity leaders, that is the real divide. Not AI adopters versus non-adopters. Disciplined value creators versus expensive experimenters.
